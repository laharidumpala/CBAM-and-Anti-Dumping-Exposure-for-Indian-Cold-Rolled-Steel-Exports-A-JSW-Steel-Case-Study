# CBAM-and-Anti-Dumping-Exposure-for-Indian-Cold-Rolled-Steel-Exports-A-JSW-Steel-Case-Study
Quantifies CBAM and anti-dumping cost exposure for Indian cold-rolled steel exports to the EU, using JSW Steel's verified BRSR emissions data. Finds a 48.8% CBAM cost saving from actual vs default emissions data, and ~€53/tonne of remaining competitiveness margin after both 2026 regulatory measures combine.


## 1. Objective and Scope

This analysis quantifies the carbon-border and trade-defense cost exposure facing
Indian cold-rolled steel (CRC) exports to the European Union in 2026, using JSW
Steel Limited's officially disclosed emissions and production data as a real,
verified case study. It addresses two questions: (1) how much does an Indian
exporter save by submitting verified actual emissions data to CBAM instead of
accepting the EU's default penalty values, and (2) once CBAM is combined with the
EU's newly imposed anti-dumping duty, does India's cold-rolled steel retain a
price advantage over EU domestic production.

**Scope.** This analysis is scoped specifically to **cold-rolled flat steel
(CRC)** — the product covered both by the EU's August 2026 anti-dumping duty and
by the Platts price data used for the landed-cost comparison. Results should not
be generalized to other steel products (hot-rolled coil, bars, structural
sections), which may carry different trade duties and CBAM benchmark values.

**Case study company.** JSW Steel Limited was selected as the sole quantified
case after evaluating Tata Steel and Jindal Steel & Power for the same purpose.
Tata Steel's India-to-EU export tonnage does not appear in any quarterly
production release examined (four separate FY2024-25 through FY2026-27 filings
checked); Jindal Steel had no independently verifiable EU-specific figure at all.
JSW Steel is named specifically and by entity in the European Commission's
anti-dumping determination, and its own FY2025-26 Integrated Report discloses
CBAM as a material transition risk with direct cost implications for its
European exports — giving it the most defensible evidentiary basis of the three.



## 2. Regulatory Background

**CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism).** Entered its definitive phase on
1 January 2026. Importers of covered goods, including steel, must purchase and
surrender CBAM certificates corresponding to the embedded carbon emissions of
imported products. Certificates are priced against the EU ETS auction-clearing
price, published quarterly by the European Commission for the 2026 compliance
year.

**Anti-dumping duty.** On 6 August 2026, the European Commission published
definitive anti-dumping duties on cold-rolled flat steel imports from five
countries, following a 10-month investigation initiated in September 2025 on a
complaint from EUROFER. Indian producers — JSW Steel Limited, JSW Steel Coated
Products Limited, and Tata Steel India — were assigned a **9.5% definitive duty**,
applied as a percentage of the CIF Union frontier price. The Commission
explicitly stated that anti-dumping measures and CBAM serve distinct legal
purposes and are not substitutes for one another, confirming that both costs
apply simultaneously to the same shipment.

**CCTS (India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme).** India's domestic compliance
carbon market. The iron and steel sectoral emission-intensity (GEI) notification
was issued in June 2026, with compliance mandated from FY2026-27 — meaning the
first three months of calendar 2026 (which fall in FY2025-26) are not yet
covered.



## 3. Data and Methodology

**Emissions source.** JSW Steel's Scope 1 emissions (60,258,642 tCO2e) are taken
directly from its official BRSR XBRL filing on NSE, FY2025-26. BRSR is an
entity-level filing under "JSW Steel Limited" specifically, not a consolidated
group filing; JSW's Ohio (US) and Piombino (Italy) operations sit in separate
legal entities. On this basis, the Scope 1 figure is inferred — not explicitly
confirmed in the filing — to represent India operations only. A cross-check
against JSW's publicly disclosed combined Scope 1+2 intensity (~2.4 tCO2/tcs) is
consistent with this inference but, given Ohio's small share of consolidated
volume (under 3%), cannot fully discriminate between an India-only and a
consolidated boundary.

**Production denominator.** 29,250,000 tonnes, JSW's disclosed India-operations
crude steel production for FY2025-26.

**SIFA construction (free allocation adjustment).** Certificates owed are
calculated as embedded emissions *minus* a free-allocation deduction (SIFA),
*then* multiplied by the certificate price — not the shortcut formula of
"emissions × price × 2.5%" that appears in some public guides, which is only
accurate for a producer sitting exactly at benchmark efficiency. SIFA is
calculated as the free-allocation share (97.5% for 2026, per the CBAM factor
under Directive 2003/87/EC) multiplied by the EU's steel product benchmark
(1.370 tCO2e/t for the BF-BOF route, per Implementing Regulation (EU)
2025/2621).

**Certificate price.** €75.36/tCO2e, the official Q1 2026 quarterly average EU
ETS auction-clearing price published by the European Commission (DG TAXUD).

**Anti-dumping duty base.** 9.5% applied to the Platts CIF Antwerp import price
for cold-rolled coil (€655/tonne, 6 August 2026).

**Domestic-import spread.** Derived from Platts' same-day assessments: €825/t
domestic ex-works Ruhr minus €655/t imported CIF Antwerp = €170/t.



## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Actual data vs. EU default values

| Scenario | CBAM cost (€/t) |
|---|---|
| JSW actual verified emissions | **€54.59** |
| EU default (illustrative estimate) | €106.58 |
| **Saving from verified data** | **48.8%** |

Submitting verified actual emissions data rather than accepting an EU default
value nearly halves JSW's CBAM certificate cost. This finding is presented as a
**floor, not a ceiling**: the illustrative default value used here (2.5 tCO2e/t
+ 10% markup) is conservative relative to the one confirmed country-specific
default located in this research — China's steel-slab default of 3.167
tCO2e/t. Under the CBAM definitive-phase rule that assigns countries lacking
their own reliable data the *highest* observed intensity among countries that
do have reliable data, India's true default — if one has not been separately
established — may sit at or above the China figure. At that anchor, the saving
from using verified data rises to **60.4%** (66.3% including the 10% 2026
markup).

### 4.2 Landed cost stack: CBAM + anti-dumping duty

| Component | €/tonne CRC |
|---|---|
| Anti-dumping duty (9.5%, EC, 6 Aug 2026) | €62.23 |
| CBAM (actual verified data, Q1 2026) | €54.59 |
| **Total regulatory cost** | **€116.81** |
| Domestic-import price spread (Platts) | €170.00 |
| **Remaining competitiveness headroom** | **€53.19** |

This is the project's central finding. Combining both EU measures — which the
Commission has confirmed operate independently and are not substitutes — yields
a total landed-cost burden of €116.81 per tonne. Against a current €170/tonne
price advantage of imported over domestic cold-rolled steel, JSW retains
approximately **€53 of headroom**: India's cost advantage narrows materially
under the compounding regulatory measures but is not eliminated, provided the
exporter uses verified rather than default emissions data.

**This headroom is a 2026 snapshot, not a stable position.** The CBAM factor —
currently 2.5%, meaning 97.5% of embedded emissions still receive free
allocation — rises to 48.5% by 2030 and 100% by 2034 as EU free allocation
phases out. Holding the anti-dumping duty and steel prices constant, the CBAM
cost line alone will grow substantially over this period, progressively
compressing and likely eliminating the €53/tonne headroom well before full
phase-in in 2034.

### 4.3 CCTS interaction

Zero CCTS deduction is the correct base case for the first quarter of calendar
2026 (which falls in FY2025-26, before the iron and steel sectoral compliance
mandate begins). For the remaining three quarters of 2026 (FY2026-27, CCTS's
first compliance year for this sector), a domestic carbon price could, in
principle, reduce CBAM liability further — modelled here at €53.99–€53.58/tonne
under illustrative ₹500/tCO2e pricing and the sector's disclosed 5.5–9.3% GEI
reduction targets. However, this deduction is **not currently available in
practice**: Article 9-equivalent recognition of CCTS as an EU-equivalent carbon
pricing scheme is unconfirmed, meaning the deduction may not be legally
permitted for any part of 2026 regardless of domestic timing.



## 5. Limitations

- **Product scope.** This analysis covers cold-rolled flat steel only. Findings
  do not extend to other steel products, which face different duties and
  potentially different CBAM benchmarks.
- **Benchmark symmetry (unresolved, material to the headline finding).** The
  1.370 tCO2e/t benchmark is applied identically to both the actual-data and
  default-value branches. It is unconfirmed whether the definitive phase
  applies a different benchmark specifically when actual data is submitted; if
  asymmetric, this would compress or reverse the 48.8% saving.
- **CN-code benchmark match (unresolved).** It is unconfirmed whether 1.370 is
  the benchmark for crude steel/hot metal generally or specifically for
  cold-rolled flat steel's CN heading. Sensitivity: each 0.01 tCO2e/t of
  benchmark value shifts cost by approximately €0.73/tonne.
- **India-specific default value not located.** No confirmed India-specific
  default value under IR 2025/2621 was found in this research; the figure used
  is an illustrative estimate, stated as likely conservative relative to the
  confirmed China anchor.
- **BRSR entity boundary.** The India-only interpretation of JSW's Scope 1
  figure is inferred from standard BRSR filing practice and weakly corroborated
  by a disclosed intensity cross-check, not confirmed directly in the filing.
- **CCTS recognition.** Whether the EU will recognize CCTS as a deductible
  carbon pricing scheme under CBAM is an open policy question, not resolved in
  this analysis.
- **Production denominator estimate.** JSW's production figure is drawn from
  company disclosures; no CBAM-relevant installation-level production split was
  available.
